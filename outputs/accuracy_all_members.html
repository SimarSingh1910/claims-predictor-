Read this before quoting any percentage If you measure "what percentage of predictions were correct" — the everyday meaning of accuracy — a model that simply says "nobody will be hospitalised" for every person scores 85.75%.

That model never identifies anybody and is useless, yet it scores in the high eighties, because hospitalisation is rare. "86% accurate" would be a true sentence and a badly misleading one. Every figure below measures something more demanding: how well the model picks out the people who actually were hospitalised.

Training and testing agree closely The drop from training to testing is under 4 percentage points in both cases. The model did not overfit — what it learned on the training members carried over to members it had never seen.

Using only 7 measurements costs almost nothing. Cutting from 44 measurements to 7 moved the test score from 46.1% to 43.4%. The seven core measurements — age, sex, blood sugar, blood pressure, BMI, kidney function and the count of abnormal findings — carry nearly all of the available signal.

One caveat that must travel with these numbers This population includes dependants — covered parents and spouses. Dependants appear in the data almost exclusively when hospitalised, because they rarely attend routine check-ups, whereas employees attend annually whether well or unwell.

A meaningful share of the 46.1% therefore reflects who attends screening rather than who is unwell. Restricted to employees only, the same measure falls to 17.6% against an 8.8% guessing line. Both figures are real; they answer different questions.
